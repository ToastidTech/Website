PWAs · 01

Why Progressive Web Apps Are Quietly Winning

A Progressive Web App sits in the sweet spot between a traditional website and a native mobile app. It can be installed on a device, work across modern browsers, load quickly, and provide an app-like experience without requiring a customer to hunt through an app store.

The business advantage

For many small businesses, the goal is not "build an app." The goal is "make this task easier for customers." A PWA can accomplish that with less friction and a single web codebase. That can mean faster iteration, simpler distribution, and fewer barriers between a customer and the tool they need.

When it makes sense

  • You need a focused tool rather than a giant platform.
  • Customers or employees need frequent access from phones or desktops.
  • You want to ship updates without waiting for app-store review cycles.
  • You care about keeping development and maintenance practical.

The important part is not the acronym. It is whether the product removes friction. If it does, the technology is doing its job.

PWAs · 02

When Should Your Business Build a PWA?

Before writing code, ask what the user is trying to accomplish. A PWA is a strong candidate when the workflow is focused, repeatable, and valuable enough that people will return to it.

Good candidates

  • Internal calculators, checklists, dashboards, and field tools.
  • Customer portals and lightweight self-service experiences.
  • Habit, wellness, productivity, or tracking applications.
  • Specialized tools that do one thing extremely well.

Probably not a PWA problem

If your product requires deep device integrations, advanced graphics, or platform-specific capabilities that the web cannot reliably provide, a native app may be the better fit. The answer should follow the problem, not the developer's favorite hammer.

PWAs · 03

From Idea to Installable App

A useful PWA typically needs four building blocks: a responsive interface, a web app manifest, service-worker capabilities where appropriate, and a reliable deployment setup. Then comes the part people skip: testing the actual workflow on the devices people will use.

The product loop

Start with one painful workflow. Build the smallest useful version. Put it in front of real users. Watch where they hesitate. Fix those points. Then add features based on evidence rather than imagination.

That approach keeps a small app small enough to maintain while still giving it room to grow. The best first release is rarely the biggest one. It is the one people actually use.

Business Audits · 01

What a Business Audit Should Really Find

A useful business technology audit should answer three questions: Where are you losing time? Where are you taking unnecessary risk? Where is technology leaving money or capacity on the table?

Look for friction

Repeated data entry, spreadsheet gymnastics, unclear ownership, manual reporting, duplicate systems, disconnected customer information, and processes that depend on one person's memory are all signals.

Turn findings into action

Every finding should lead toward a decision: keep it, simplify it, automate it, replace it, or eliminate it. If an audit cannot tell you what to do next, it is analysis without a steering wheel.

Business Audits · 02

The 5 Areas I Look at First

When evaluating a business, I start broad enough to see the system but narrow enough to find actionable problems.

  • People: Who does the work, and where are they overloaded?
  • Process: What steps happen repeatedly, and where do handoffs break?
  • Technology: Which tools help, which tools duplicate each other, and which are barely used?
  • Data: Where does important information live, and can people trust it?
  • Customer experience: Where does the customer encounter unnecessary friction?

These five areas tend to expose the difference between a business that is merely busy and one that is building scalable operations.

Automation · 01

Stop Automating Broken Processes

If a workflow is confusing when a human performs it, automating it usually makes the confusion happen faster. Before choosing a tool, document the current process from trigger to outcome.

Fix first

Remove unnecessary approvals. Eliminate duplicate entry. Define who owns each step. Decide what information is actually required. Then identify the repetitive pieces that remain.

Automation should reduce decisions, clicks, waiting, and handoffs. If it adds another dashboard for employees to babysit, you may have created a new problem wearing a productivity costume.

Automation · 02

What Should You Automate First?

Start with work that is frequent, predictable, rules-based, and annoying. Those characteristics create a strong return because the automation runs often and requires little judgment.

Great first targets

  • Moving information between systems.
  • Generating routine reports or notifications.
  • Sorting incoming requests into known categories.
  • Creating repetitive documents from structured data.
  • Scheduling reminders and follow-ups.

Track the hours saved, errors avoided, and response time improved. If you cannot measure the improvement, you will have a harder time deciding what to automate next.

AI & Strategy · 01

AI for Small Business Without the Hype

AI is most useful when it removes a bottleneck. That might mean summarizing information, drafting repetitive content, classifying requests, extracting data, helping employees find answers, or assisting with a workflow that previously required several manual steps.

Start with the work

Do not begin by asking, "Where can I put AI?" Begin by asking, "What takes too much time, happens too often, or requires more manual effort than it should?" Then evaluate whether AI is actually the right tool.

Sometimes the answer is AI. Sometimes it is a simple automation. Sometimes the answer is deleting the process entirely. Good strategy is knowing the difference.

AI & Strategy · 02

Build, Buy, or Automate?

There are three common paths when a business has a technology problem. Buy an existing product, automate an existing workflow, or build something custom.

Buy when

The problem is common, mature products already solve it, and customization is not central to your competitive advantage.

Automate when

You already have the right tools but people are repeatedly moving information, triggering actions, or producing predictable outputs by hand.

Build when

The workflow is specific to your business, existing tools create more friction than they remove, or the solution itself can become a valuable product.

The cheapest option is not always the least expensive. Total cost includes subscriptions, labor, errors, maintenance, and the cost of making people work around a bad system.
